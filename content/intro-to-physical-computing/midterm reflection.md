---
i thought i failed the ask, but i didn't. 

granted, i was not able to achieve the desired 'interactivity' (due to my misdoings with the fsr), but i: 

- made a system with physical controls.
- kept people engaged. 
- understood all the technical concepts. 
- made people enjoy. 

i do <mark>wish that i worked in a team</mark>. i was envious of seeing people <mark>split up their work</mark>, and <mark>achieve something greater than what they could individually</mark> produce. 

i regret not scaling down my project. this prevented my experimentation with testing, physical-interaction-design, and actually being able to see how people interacted with the thing i made. in the next one, i shall scale it down, to be able to <mark>do a small thing really well</mark>. it would also help to account for equal amounts of time for all the things that we are asked to do (and not let circuitry take up all my time). 

---
throughout this midterm-period, i've managed to <mark>help multiple people with their circuits & their programs</mark>. that, to me, was a <mark>richer learning experience than making my own project</mark>. it <mark>forced me to grapple with the basics</mark>, and do so with a high level of confidence since someone else was desperately relying on me. 

i am happy with the extra hours that i invested at the beginning of this course. this is clear evidence for me to <mark>go about everything in my life with high amounts of curiosity</mark> — i am on the floor to learn. 

---
<mark>i love electricity</mark>. it is like falling in love with infinite possibilities all over again — exactly how it felt when i first stumbled upon code. 

now that i've gone through this process with a medium before, i can tell that <mark>the essence of electricity is different than the essence of code</mark>— and i am far away from understanding what it is yet. i also know that i can only arrive at it via about 4-years worth of experimentation (atleast that's how much it costed me with code). 

another thing with electricity, or more fittingly — 'physical' computing — is the <mark>tangibility</mark>. i can feel something there (see [[tangibility makes magic more plausible]]). i don't know what it is, but the feeling is strong & attractive. <mark>there is just something different about having a thing interact with you outside the flat premise of the screen</mark>. 

simple things — such as seeing input resulting in output, and being in a loop of feedback with a computing machine (where something you do changes the way something else is) — are powerful experiences.

---
my love & fascination for electricity scares me. 

i fell in love with something that i wasn't academically trained for (code), and struggled to find an economically feasible way to exercise my wonder. 

it feels like the exact same will happen with tangible things (and electricity) — i will have this thing that i love & really want to do, but will struggle to find economically-feasible spaces to do so. i don't want to let that happen again. 

i'm 25. every day i spent split — between being a design-researcher / product-designer & computational-artist / researcher — was torture. i need to exit this space with a way to exercise my curiosity. 

<mark>i don't want to make a lot of money. i just want to be able to keep doing this thing that i love</mark>. please tell me how to; tom.
